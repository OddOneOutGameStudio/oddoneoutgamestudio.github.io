Level 05 · Slot game · Stake Engine

DOPAMINE.exe

Kinga · solo · 2026
Rec
Storybook run · DOPAMINE.exeLoop · 16 s

An original 5×4, 20-line slot built for Stake Engine, the platform where independent creators publish casino games for Stake. The concept: corrupted software, a dopamine loop, degen internet culture. The interface degrades as you win — a DOPAMINE METER climbs from 0 to 100 and the machine goes from calm green to red alarms, glitches and chaos.

The scatter is a red button labelled DO NOT PRESS. The wild is WILD.exe. The bonus is DOPAMINE OVERLOAD: free spins with multiplier wilds, ×2 to ×50. A lab goblin watches from a tank next to the reels and goes through five moods from bored to manic. The spin button evolves from STIMULATE to MORE to DO IT.

Two SDKs, learned from zero. The math lives in the Python Math SDK: symbol strips, paytable, bonus logic, an RTP optimiser (in Rust) tuned to exactly 0.965, a 5000× win cap, and the books of pre-simulated outcomes that the engine actually serves.

The frontend is the point. The Web SDK (Svelte 5 + PIXI 8) ships a generic mining theme; almost all of it was replaced with hand-built HTML overlays: the machine frame with its alarm sequence, the control bar, the bonus panels, the goblin rig with a typewriter speech bubble, and eleven symbols drawn as CSS art and rasterised with headless Chrome. The result runs end to end in the storybook, including a $5,000 win-cap book.

Level 06 · Immersive party game · Halloween

Opuszczona Nałęczowska

Game night · 30 Oct 2026
Phone · projectors · TV
Chapter I · The ball beginsEveryone gets their circle

An original story game for about twenty people, one evening, one real apartment. Part theatre, part escape room, part detective story, part 1920s casino. Guests don't watch the plot — they play it. Each one is the ghost of a guest from a masked ball on 30 October 1926 that ended with the tenement house on fire. Exactly a hundred years later the Croupier comes back for the souls signed away on an IOU that night. The only way out is to name who set the fire, where and with what. The catch: the arsonist is among the players and doesn't know it.

Three layers, one game. The phone is each player's private command centre — nothing to install, just a QR code from the invitation on the TV. Two projectors turn the living-room walls into the rooms of the house: cellar, ballroom, bedroom, attic, with synchronised film scenes between chapters. The TV speaks with the voice of the house, shows the game board, the case files and the IOU clock counting down two hours. Every screen and every phone is live, in the same second.

What players do. Circles: four teams (hearts, diamonds, clubs, spades) revealed only on your phone, so you have to find each other in the room. A race to the safe on every floor: ciphers, a portrait-wall riddle and a ghost's question that only informants from rival circles can answer. Personal missions closed with codes hidden in the flat or another player's seal. An investigation with private clues you can keep, gift, sell or trade, and a prosecution file on your phone. The Croupier's casino: one-on-one duels for gold or a shot, on a custom art-déco deck. And a phone from beyond: the cellar switchboard calls random guests.

Penalties and finale. After every floor the three poorest souls go on stage: a ten-second old-cinema countdown, then karaoke with lyrics on the TV, a charleston or live dubbing of a silent film. The finale is a shared blackjack table projected on the wall — every guest picks a seat on their phone, all hands visible. If at least two circles name the arsonist, the clock stuck at 23:47 for a century starts again and the guests leave the ball. If not, the ball goes on forever.

Built from scratch. Script, mechanics and art direction are original: sepia portraits of every guest as they were in 1926, generated scenes with each player's face, gilded premium cards, narrator recordings. It all runs on an ordinary laptop in the home network, and the host runs the evening from a control panel.
